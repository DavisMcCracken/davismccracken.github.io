## The question

Recommendation algorithms decide most of what we read and watch online, and they're tuned for one thing: engagement. My capstone, *Algorithms and the Mind: An Interdisciplinary Approach to Digital Well-Being* (November 2024), asks what that trade-off costs us, and why no single field can answer it on its own.

## Three lenses

- **Technology.** Feeds moved from human editors with journalistic standards to opaque systems ranked on clicks and time-on-site. That shift rewards sensational and polarizing content (DeVito, 2016).
- **Psychology.** Algorithms feed confirmation bias, and the "triple-filter bubble" shows how personal bias, social networks and technical filters reinforce each other (Geschke, Lorenz & Holtz, 2019). Algorithm-shaped social comparison can lift or damage self-evaluation (Kang & Liu, 2019), and people change their own behaviour to win better recommendations (Taylor & Choi, 2022).
- **Sociology.** Platforms built on friend networks and personalized feeds show stronger echo chambers (Cinelli et al., 2021). Misinformation spreads fastest when it confirms what people already believe (Onifade, 2023).

## What helps

1. **Critical media literacy.** People who understand how algorithms work are more likely to fact-check and to support moderation (Chung, 2023).
2. **Mindfulness.** Noticing the design patterns built to capture attention makes it easier to set limits on them (Harris, 2017).
3. **Different curation.** Crowdsourced trust ratings track professional fact-checkers closely without adding partisan bias (Epstein, Pennycook & Rand, 2020).

## Why it matters to my work

I build systems that decide what people see, even if it's only a dashboard or a donor list. This project is why I care about transparency in those systems, and about tools that serve the people using them rather than steer them.
